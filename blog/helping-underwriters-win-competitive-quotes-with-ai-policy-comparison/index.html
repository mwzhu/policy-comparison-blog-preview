Helping Underwriters Win Competitive Quotes with AI Policy Comparison

How We Cut Insurance Policy Comparison from Hours to Minutes

Why Policy Comparison

Global insurance is a $9.15 trillion market that runs on long documents people still compare by hand. Commercial insurance, the coverage businesses buy, has some of the longest and most customized of these documents.

During the commercial insurance lifecycle (figure 1), a business buys commercial insurance through a broker. The broker shops the account to insurance companies (carriers). At each carrier, an underwriter decides whether to offer coverage, at what price, and on what terms. Then they send back a quote.

Comparing the offers is traditionally the broker’s job. A carrier that wants to win an account from the incumbent can make that job easier by doing the comparison itself and sending it with the quote. Brokers want to see the differences at a glance, and some won’t consider a batch of quotes that arrives without a comparison.

Done by hand, that means reading two policies that can each run to hundreds of pages. The underwriter has to find the provisions that matter for this kind of business, then write down how they differ. That is specialist work for a single account, and a broker’s renewal batch can hold hundreds of accounts.

On top of the hours of manual document comparison work, mistakes are expensive. If comparisons incorrectly state a policy’s offer, both the carrier and broker are exposed to an errors-and-omissions (E&O) claim, which is liability for a professional mistake.

Automating this policy comparison workflow reliably saves time and reduces risk for mistakes.

We go into what makes this challenging in the next section.

Insurance Domain Challenges

There are a couple reasons why the insurance domain specifically makes document information extraction more challenging than traditional AI extraction. The orchestration of multiple insurance forms working together, often with missing or conflicting domain-specific information, makes a simple LLM wrapper prone to failure. Here we take a look at 3 of the largest challenges:

1. Policies as Multiple Forms

An insurance policy document is a bundle of forms, usually starting with a base coverage form. Endorsements then add, delete, or replace wording in the base form or in each other. The combination of multiple endorsements on top of the base form makes the document understanding complex, as an LLM can’t just go page by page but must take in all forms holistically.

As an example, suppose a carrier’s base form excludes injuries at swimming pools. Endorsement A replaces that exclusion with a narrower one that applies only when no lifeguard is on duty. Endorsement B caps pool injury claims at $50,000 per incident. Read page by page, the AI reports three separate facts and misses how they work together. Read together, the real coverage is clear: pool injuries are covered up to $50,000 per incident when a lifeguard is on duty.

2. Missing Forms

Policies usually contain a schedule of forms, which lists the relevant forms (base form and endorsements) that policy uses. However, most policy documents don’t include the actual forms themselves, so underwriters must fetch those forms manually before writing their quote. Automating this process with software requires a comprehensive form library and robust form identification logic (more on this in the product components section).

3. Conflicting Forms

Individual forms with the same name can contain different or conflicting information. For example, two carriers can both attach a form titled “Assault or Battery,” but the actual insurance policy within these forms are different. One policy may specify no coverage, while the other may specify a $100,000 sublimit.

It isn’t enough to match clauses by form title when creating quotes. The software system must look into the specific form content and understand the domain nuance to correctly inform the comparisons between coverages.

What We Built

Policy Comparison is a tool we built for the underwriting team of a specialty insurance carrier. An underwriter uploads the competitor’s policy, along with their own quote and policy forms. A few minutes later they get:

  1. A comparison table. It has one row for each question a broker asks about this kind of business, such as additional insured, assault and battery, or hot work for roofers.
  2. Up to three highlights. These are the places where the rules can show, from cited rows, that the carrier’s offer is better.
  3. A source link on every cell. Each one opens the PDF with a box around the quoted words.
  4. A Word and PDF report. The underwriter can edit it, then attach it to the quote that goes to the broker.

Product Components

1. Document Extraction

The first step in the product workflow after insurance carriers upload their policy documents is extracting insurance information out of these documents. Extraction turns each document into structured, cited facts in three steps.

  1. Check the text first: Before anything reads a page, we check whether its text layer is usable. Healthy pages are read directly, while garbled or scanned pages go to Gemini to read an image of the page.
  2. Dual extraction: To extract insurance information from the documents, we use two readers. A rule-based reader knows about twenty common declaration layouts and reads values such as premium, fees, deductibles, and limits exactly. Model readers run in parallel for everything templates can’t handle, such as coverages, clauses, and exclusions.
  3. Merge conflicts: A value the rule-based reader read off the page, with a citation, always wins. The models fill in everything else.

2. Policy Comparison

Once the insurance information is extracted from the documents, we use this to create a policy comparison table. Users can view a side-by-side table that compares the two carriers’ policies on a set of comparison criteria for their type of business.

The creation of this comparison involves two steps:

  1. Define the rows. For each type of business, we predefine a set of comparison criteria. Each criterion is one row, with written rules for what belongs in it. Defining the criteria up front, instead of letting an LLM invent the rows, keeps every table consistent and reliable.
  2. Fill the rows from both policies. This involves calculating net coverage across base forms and endorsements for each carrier policy, oftentimes standardizing information across documents with different formats and labels.

3. Policy Highlights

At the top of the policy comparison report, we surface the top 3 most important highlights for an easy view of a carrier’s strongest coverage advantages.

Like policy comparison table rows, we chose to implement highlights deterministically over an LLM generation approach. We did this for 3 main reasons:

  1. Table consistency: Each highlight is built from information visible in the comparison table with citations, so it can’t claim something the table doesn’t show.
  2. Repeatability: Reports generate the same highlights every time, making them more reliable in the user experience and developer testing.
  3. Fast and free regeneration: Users can choose to hide certain table rows when downloading reports. In this case deterministically generating highlights avoids the more expensive and slower LLM highlight generation process.

4. Citations

Underwriters trust the policy comparison table and highlights because they can check it. Every cell has source links, and clicking one opens the PDF with a box around the quoted words.

Citations are created during document extraction. The rule-based and model readers record the page and exact quote for each value they extract. Using this information, we draw the bounding boxes for the citations by searching the page’s own text for the exact quote. If there is exactly one match, we draw the box there. If there are none or several, we use nearby text on the page or a vision model to pick the right match. As a fallback, if it’s still unclear, the link opens the right page with no box, so we never highlight the wrong field.

5. Forms Library

As mentioned in the “Insurance Domain Challenges” section in point 2, a quote often lists forms that it doesn’t have in the uploaded documents. To address this limitation, we built the forms library, a shared catalog of insurance forms that maps form titles, numbers and editions with their document PDF and wording.

By storing all the forms we have previously seen, our extraction pipeline can handle missing forms from user document uploads by automatically searching through the comprehensive forms library and finding the correct document. Comparisons stay complete even when quotes don’t include all necessary documents, and underwriters don’t have to find and upload each missing form by hand.

Forms get ingested into the library in 3 ways: automatic uploads synced with the carrier’s document storage system (i.e. Microsoft Sharepoint), forms found in policies that get processed through our policy comparison, and forms underwriters manually upload to the library.

Evals

For an AI product in insurance, evals are how we prove the output is trustworthy enough to put in front of a broker. Every day, the eval regenerates a set of real comparisons with production code and scores the finished tables against answer keys written by insurance reviewers. Handling Private Data

Since we’re dealing with sensitive client data, we can’t keep real customer policies in our code repository. The usual way to build evals (storing test examples alongside the code in git) would leak them.

To tackle this problem, we store our eval cases with the real policies and answer keys in private storage inside our controlled environment. Our eval code runs inside that same environment each day, reads the cases at run time, and sends out only scores and counts. Customer documents never end up in git, CI logs, or chat.

Conclusion

Underwriters can now upload their quote and a competitor’s policy (often 150 pages or more) and get back a cited, broker-ready comparison 40x faster. Work that would have taken an underwriter 4 hours by hand can now be completed in under 10 minutes.

That speed makes bulk quotes practical, as a carrier can answer a broker’s entire renewal batch with a comparison attached to every quote. At our insurance carrier client’s goal of 100 bulk-quote comparisons a week, work that would have taken 400 underwriter-hours, or roughly 10 full-time underwriters, can now be completed by one operator.

It also helps reduce coverage errors, as every cell links to its source, so an underwriter can check any claim in one click before the quote goes out. Catching mistakes before sending means fewer late corrections with the broker.
